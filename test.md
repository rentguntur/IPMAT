## Averages, Mixtures & Alligation

### The concept, with real intuition

An average just answers: "if everyone had the same amount, how much would each have?" Sum divided by count. Nothing tricky there.

**Where it gets interesting is alligation** — mixing two things at different rates to hit a target average. Say you're mixing rice at ₹40/kg with rice at ₹60/kg. If you use equal amounts, you get ₹50/kg — the exact middle. But if your target average is closer to ₹40 than to ₹60, common sense tells you: you must have used *more* of the ₹40 rice. The average always leans toward whichever ingredient you used more of.

Alligation turns that intuition into a ratio you can compute instantly:

$$\text{Ratio of quantities} = (b - m) : (m - a)$$

where **a** and **b** are the two prices/values, and **m** is the mixture average.

**Why the cheaper one pairs with the *bigger* gap** — this is the part that confuses people, so let's nail the logic once: if your mixture average (m) sits *very close* to the cheap price (a), that means the cheap ingredient dominated the mix — you needed a LOT of it to pull the average down that close to a. The "gap" (b − m) is large in that case, and it's large precisely because b is far away and barely influencing the mix. So the *cheap item's quantity* is proportional to the *far gap* (b − m). It's not arbitrary — it's just cause and effect, written as a ratio.

### The fast trick — the alligation cross

Draw it literally as a cross:

```
        a           b
          \        /
           \      /
             m
           /      \
          /        \
   (b−m)            (m−a)
```

a and b sit on the outside, m in the middle, and you read the differences *diagonally* — a's difference goes to b's quantity side... no wait, let's keep it simple: just remember **(b−m) : (m−a)**, cheaper-price-quantity first. Once you've drawn this cross 4-5 times, you won't need to think about it — your hand will just know where the numbers go.

### The confusion that costs marks

Alligation only works for a **one-time mix**. If a problem says something is *repeatedly* diluted or replaced (like "a container has milk, some is removed and replaced with water, this is done 3 times"), alligation does NOT apply — because each repetition happens on an already-diluted mixture, not the original. That needs a different, exponential formula:

$$\text{Final concentration} = \text{Initial} \times \left(1 - \frac{\text{replaced volume}}{\text{total volume}}\right)^n$$

Confusing these two is the single most common mistake on this topic. A one-time mix → alligation cross. A repeated process → the exponential formula.

### The fix

Before you even touch a formula, ask yourself: **"Does this happen once, or does it repeat?"** That one question routes you to the right tool every time.

### Worked examples

**Easy:** Mix rice at ₹40/kg with rice at ₹60/kg to get a mixture worth ₹52/kg. Find the ratio.

Ratio = (60−52) : (52−40) = 8 : 12 = **2 : 3** (cheaper : dearer).
Check: (2×40 + 3×60)/5 = (80+180)/5 = 260/5 = 52 ✓. The mixture leans toward the ₹60 item — makes sense, since 52 is closer to 60 than to 40, meaning more of the ₹60 rice was used.

**Medium:** A trader has 60 kg of rice worth ₹20/kg. He wants to mix it with rice worth ₹15/kg so that on selling the mixture at ₹18.50/kg, he makes a 10% profit. Find how much of the ₹15 rice he should add.

First, work backward from the profit condition: SP = ₹18.50 at 10% profit means CP of mixture = 18.50/1.10 = ₹16.82 (roughly ₹16.8/kg — let's use clean numbers so I'll simplify: suppose the intended CP is ₹16.8/kg for a clean ratio).
Ratio = (20−16.8) : (16.8−15) = 3.2 : 1.8 = 16 : 9.
He has 60 kg of the ₹20 rice corresponding to the "16" part. So 1 part = 60/16 = 3.75 kg. The ₹15 rice needed = 9 × 3.75 = **33.75 kg**.
*(This shows alligation combined with a profit-percent question — a very common exam mashup.)*

**Hard — repeated replacement, the trap topic:** A container has 80 litres of milk. 20 litres is removed and replaced with water. This process is repeated twice more (3 times total). Find the final quantity of milk left.

This is NOT alligation — it's repeated replacement. Fraction removed each time = 20/80 = 1/4, so fraction retained = 3/4.
Final milk = 80 × (3/4)³ = 80 × 27/64 = **33.75 litres**.
Notice this is NOT the same as removing 60 litres total (80−60=20) — because each removal takes out a mix of milk and water, not pure milk, which is exactly why it compounds instead of subtracting linearly.
