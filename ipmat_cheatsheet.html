Section one

Quantitative ability

Percentages

Understand it: what a percentage actually is

"Percent" means "per hundred." 25% just means 25 out of every 100 — it's a ratio put on a common scale so different things become comparable. "45 out of 60" is hard to compare to "38 out of 50" until you convert both to percentages (75% and 76%) — now they're on the same scale.

x% of y = (x/100) × y

Every percentage question is really just this one relationship (part, whole, percentage) viewed from a different angle: find the percentage, find the part, or find the whole. Same equation, different unknown.

Percentage change is different from "percentage of" — it asks how much something grew or shrank relative to where it started:

% change = (New − Old) / Old × 100

The denominator is always the ORIGINAL value — never the new one. This is where marks get lost, because using the wrong base "feels" fine when you're moving fast.

Why "up 50%, down 50%" isn't back to zero: ₹100 up 50% → ₹150. Now ₹150 down 50% → you lose 50% of 150, which is 75, leaving ₹75 — not ₹100. The second move was calculated on a bigger pile than the first, so it doesn't cancel out. This is why any "up X%, down X%" sequence always ends in a net loss.

You can get this net effect in one line instead of two steps:

Net % change = a + b + (ab/100)

Check: a=+50, b=−50 → 50 − 50 + (50×−50)/100 = 0 − 25 = −25%. ₹100 at −25% = ₹75. Matches.

Trick

Memorize these fraction equivalents cold, don't calculate them:

1/2 = 50%1/7 ≈ 14.28%
1/3 = 33.33%1/8 = 12.5%
1/4 = 25%1/9 = 11.11%
1/5 = 20%1/11 ≈ 9.09%
1/6 ≈ 16.67%1/12 ≈ 8.33%
Confusion

A 50% increase followed by a 50% decrease does not return you to the original — net is −25%, because the base changed between steps.

Fix

Say "% change = (New − Old)/Old" in your head before plugging in numbers, until it's automatic.

Try it — A number is increased by 25%, then decreased by 20%. Net % change?
25 − 20 + (25×−20)/100 = 5 − 5 = 0%. Worth memorizing as a recurring shape in DI sets.

Profit, loss & discount

Understand it: why the multiplier method works

Profit% is always measured against Cost Price: Profit% = (SP−CP)/CP × 100. Discount, on the other hand, is always applied to the Marked Price, not the cost price — MP is the "sticker price" before any discount, and SP is what's actually paid after discount.

Here's the intuition for combining multiple percentage moves: instead of tracking rupee amounts through each step, track the multiplying factor. A 40% markup means the price is multiplied by 1.40. A 25% discount means what's left is multiplied by 0.75 (you keep 75%, not lose 75%). Since these are sequential operations on the same starting value, you just multiply the factors: 1.40 × 0.75 = 1.05, meaning the final price is 105% of the original cost — a 5% profit. No need to compute intermediate MP or discount amounts at all.

Confusion

"SP is 20% more than CP" and "CP is 20% less than SP" sound reversible but are not the same statement mathematically.

Fix

Fix CP = 100 whenever only percentages are given, and derive every other value as an actual number from there.

Try it — Marked 40% above cost, 25% discount given. Profit or loss %?
1.40 × 0.75 = 1.05 → 5% profit.

Averages, mixtures & alligation

Understand it: what alligation is really doing

A simple average treats all items equally. But when you mix unequal quantities of two things (say, cheap rice and expensive rice), the resulting average price leans toward whichever quantity is bigger. Alligation is a shortcut that captures this "leaning" directly from the numbers, without setting up algebra.

Ratio of quantities = (b − m) : (m − a)

Here a and b are the two prices, m is the mixture average. Notice the ratio pairs the cheaper item's quantity with the gap on the expensive side (b − m) — because the closer the average sits to a price, the more of that item must be in the mix. A bigger gap on one side means more weight is needed on the corresponding item to pull the average that far.

This only works for a single, one-time mix. If something is repeatedly diluted or replaced (like replacing part of a solution with water, again and again), you need a different, exponential formula instead — because each repetition acts on an already-changed mixture, not the original.

Fix

Before reaching for alligation, check: one-time mix, or repeated replacement? Pick the matching tool.

Try it — Mix ₹40/kg rice with ₹60/kg rice to get ₹52/kg. Ratio?
(60−52):(52−40) = 8:12 = 2:3 (cheaper:dearer). Check: (2×40+3×60)/5 = 52 ✓

Ratio, proportion & partnership

Understand it: why time matters as much as money

A partnership isn't just about how much money each partner put in — it's about how much "capital-time" each partner contributed, because ₹10,000 invested for 12 months has done twice the work of ₹10,000 invested for only 6 months. That's why profit splits according to (capital × time), not capital alone. Think of it like renting out money: the longer it's "working" in the business, the bigger the share it earns.

Confusion

Forgetting to multiply by time when partners invest for different durations — comparing raw capital instead.

Fix

Write a quick "Capital × Months" table before computing any partnership ratio, every time.

Try it — A invests ₹5000 for 12 months, B ₹6000 for 8 months. Profit ratio?
A: 5000×12=60,000. B: 6000×8=48,000. Ratio = 5:4.

Time, speed & distance

Understand it: relative speed and the train-length trick

When two things move, what matters for "closing the gap" between them is their relative speed — how fast the distance between them is changing. If they move toward each other, their speeds add (both are closing the gap). If they move the same direction, one speed is subtracted from the other (only the speed difference closes the gap).

For a train crossing something, picture the front of the train as a single dot. That dot must travel from the start of the object to its end — plus the entire length of the train itself has to clear the object too. So total distance = train length + object length (which is 0 for a pole, since a pole has no length of its own).

Trick

km/h → m/s: ×5/18. m/s → km/h: ×18/5.

Fix

For every train question, ask "what total distance does the front of the train travel?" before computing.

Try it — 150m train crosses a 250m platform in 20s. Speed in km/h?
Distance = 400m. Speed = 400/20 = 20 m/s → 20×18/5 = 72 km/h.

Time & work

Understand it: why we use rates, not days

Days-to-finish and "amount of work per day" are inverses of each other. If A finishes a job in 12 days, A completes 1/12 of the job every day — that's A's rate. Rates ADD when people work together (their combined daily output is the sum of individual outputs), but the number of DAYS does not add or average in any simple way. That's why every time-and-work problem should be converted to rates first.

The LCM trick is just a convenience: instead of saying "the whole job = 1," say "the whole job = 36 units" (the LCM of the given day-counts). Now everyone's daily rate is a whole number of units, not an awkward fraction — same math, easier arithmetic.

Confusion

"Twice as efficient" means half the time, not double — efficiency and time move in opposite directions.

Try it — A: 12 days, B: 18 days. Work together 4 days, then A leaves. Days left for B?
LCM=36. A's rate=3/day, B's rate=2/day, combined=5/day. 4 days → 20 units done, 16 remain. B alone: 16/2 = 8 more days.

Simple & compound interest

Understand it: why compound interest grows faster

Simple interest is calculated on the original principal every single time — the interest itself never earns more interest. Compound interest, though, adds each period's interest back into the principal, so the next period's interest is calculated on a slightly bigger amount. That "interest on interest" is exactly what makes CI grow faster than SI, and the gap between them widens over time.

For exactly 2 years, that extra "interest on interest" amount happens to equal P×(R/100)² — a neat shortcut derived directly from expanding the compound interest formula, so you don't need to calculate the full CI when only the difference is asked.

Confusion

"Compounded half-yearly" means double T and halve R before applying the formula.

Try it — CI − SI on ₹8000 for 2 years at 5% p.a.?
8000 × (5/100)² = 8000 × 0.0025 = ₹20.

Number system

Understand it: why unit digits repeat in cycles

When you multiply a number repeatedly, only the unit digit of the previous result affects the unit digit of the next one (e.g., ...3 × anything only depends on that 3, not the rest of the number). Because there are only 10 possible unit digits, the pattern of unit digits as you raise a number to higher and higher powers MUST eventually repeat — and for digits like 2, 3, 7, 8 that cycle repeats every 4 powers. Once you know the cycle, you don't need to compute the actual (huge) power — you only need to know where in the 4-step cycle your exponent lands.

Trick

Find exponent mod (cycle length), use 4 instead of remainder 0, then read off the cycle position.

Try it — Unit digit of 7⁸⁵?
Cycle of 7: 7,9,3,1 (length 4). 85 mod 4 = 1 → position 1 = 7.

Algebra & quadratics

Understand it: where sum and product of roots come from

If a quadratic ax²+bx+c=0 has roots p and q, then it can be written as a(x−p)(x−q) = 0. Expanding that gives ax² − a(p+q)x + apq = 0. Comparing this to the original ax²+bx+c, you can match up coefficients: −a(p+q) = b, so p+q = −b/a, and apq = c, so pq = c/a. These aren't arbitrary rules to memorize — they fall directly out of expanding the factored form, which is why they always hold for any quadratic.

Trick

Try factoring first before reaching for the quadratic formula — it's faster whenever the numbers are clean.

Try it — Roots of x²−7x+k=0 are in ratio 2:5. Find k.
Roots = 2t, 5t. Sum = 7t = 7 → t=1. Roots = 2, 5. k = product = 10.

Geometry & mensuration

Understand it: why area scales by k² and volume by k³

Area is built from two lengths multiplied together (like base × height), so if every length in a shape scales by k, the area — a product of two such lengths — scales by k × k = k². Volume is built from three lengths multiplied together, so it scales by k × k × k = k³. This is a general geometric fact, not specific to any one shape — it's why doubling a sphere's radius multiplies its volume by 8, not 2.

Confusion

Cone surface area uses slant height; cone volume uses vertical height — easy to mix up.

Try it — Sphere radius is doubled. Volume increases by what factor?
Volume ∝ r³. Doubling r → 8× the volume.

Data interpretation

Understand it: DI is a reading-speed test wearing a math costume

The actual arithmetic in most DI questions is simple — the real bottleneck is finding the right numbers in a dense table or chart fast. That's why reading the question BEFORE looking at the data pays off: you scan with a specific target in mind ("I need the March figure for Region B") instead of trying to absorb the entire dataset, most of which you'll never use.

Fix

Set a hard time cap per DI set (5–6 minutes); if it isn't yielding answers, mark it and move on.

Try it — Pie chart: Marketing = 25% of a ₹12,00,000 budget. Marketing budget?
25% = 1/4. 12,00,000/4 = ₹3,00,000.
Section two

Verbal ability

Reading comprehension

Understand it: why "questions first" actually works

RC isn't testing whether you enjoyed the passage — it's testing whether you can locate and interpret specific information quickly. If you read the whole passage first without knowing what you'll be asked, you absorb a lot of detail you won't need and forget details you will. Reading the questions first turns your passage-reading into a targeted search, which is both faster and more accurate.

Confusion

Options that are factually true but never actually stated in the passage (out-of-scope trap).

Fix

For every option ask "can I point to the exact line that supports this?" If not, eliminate it.

Try it — "Although the committee approved the budget unanimously, several members privately expressed reservations." What can be inferred?
Public agreement didn't fully reflect private opinion. Traps: "was rejected" (not stated), "opposed it publicly" (contradicts "unanimously").

Para-jumbles

Understand it: why mandatory pairs are the key

A paragraph has internal logic — sentences reference each other through pronouns ("this," "it," "they") and connectors ("however," "therefore"). A sentence that starts with "This" is grammatically dependent on something mentioned before it, so it can never be the opening sentence, and it must sit right after whatever it's referring to. Finding these dependencies (mandatory pairs) narrows the possible orderings far faster than trying to "feel out" the right sequence from scratch.

Fix

Use the answer options to eliminate — check which options are structurally impossible first, rather than building the paragraph blind.

Try it — (1) This led to a decline in local employment. (2) The factory closed in 2010 due to rising costs. (3) Workers had to migrate to nearby cities. (4) Government subsidies failed to revive it. Order?
2-4-1-3. Sentence 1 starts with "This" → can't be the opener. 2 introduces the closure, 4 extends it, 1 states the consequence, 3 follows as the further effect.

Vocabulary

Understand it: why roots beat memorized word lists

Most English vocabulary is built from a fairly small set of Latin and Greek roots. Learning that "bene-" means good and "mal-" means bad doesn't just teach you two words — it gives you a decoding tool for dozens of unfamiliar words you'll meet for the first time in the exam (benevolent, malevolent, malfunction, benefactor). This is a far better use of memory than memorizing isolated word-meaning pairs.

Fix

Check the connotation of the original word (positive/negative/neutral) first, and eliminate any option with a mismatched connotation.

Try it — PRODIGAL is to WASTEFUL as FRUGAL is to ______?
Thrifty — the relationship is a synonym pair, applied to the second word.

Grammar & sentence correction

Understand it: why the subject can hide from you

Subject-verb agreement depends only on the TRUE subject of the sentence — but English often places extra descriptive phrases between the subject and the verb, and those phrases can contain a noun that "sounds" like the subject even though it isn't. In "Each of the students have submitted," the true subject is "Each" (singular), while "students" is just part of a prepositional phrase describing which "each." Reading quickly, your ear locks onto the nearest noun ("students") instead of the real subject.

Fix

Physically cross out prepositional phrases before checking subject-verb agreement.

Try it — Find the error: "Each of the students have submitted their assignment."
"Have" should be "has" — subject is "Each" (singular).
Section three

Logical reasoning

Seating arrangement & puzzles

Understand it: why the most restrictive clue goes first

Every clue eliminates some fraction of possible arrangements. A vague clue ("X is somewhere left of Y") eliminates relatively few possibilities, while a precise clue ("X sits exactly between Y and Z") pins things down immediately. If you process vague clues first, you often build a partial picture that later gets contradicted, forcing a restart. Starting with the most restrictive clue avoids most of that backtracking.

Confusion

In circular arrangements, "left" and "right" flip depending on whether people face in or out.

Try it — 5 in a row: A immediately left of B; C at an end; D exactly in the middle. Where can E sit?
D=3 (middle). C=1 or 5. If C=1, remaining seats 2,4,5 for A,B,E — the A-B block only fits at 4,5. So A=4, B=5, and E=2.

Blood relations

Understand it: why drawing beats holding it in your head

Relationship chains ("my grandfather's only son's daughter") stack multiple hops, and each hop is easy to lose track of mentally. A quick family tree externalizes that stacking — you're no longer relying on working memory to hold three or four relationship links at once, just reading them off a drawing.

Fix

Mark gender-unknown people with a "?" until a clue explicitly confirms it.

Try it — "She is the daughter of my grandfather's only son." How is she related to Ravi (male)?
Grandfather's only son = Ravi's father. Her being his daughter makes her Ravi's sister.

Syllogisms

Understand it: "necessarily true" versus "commonly true"

Syllogisms test pure logical validity, not real-world likelihood. A conclusion is valid only if it holds in EVERY possible arrangement consistent with the given statements — not just the most "obvious" or common one. Drawing every possible Venn diagram configuration (not just the first one you imagine) is what reveals whether a conclusion truly always follows, or just often seems to.

Confusion

"Some A are B" does not imply "Some A are not B." "All A are B" does not reverse to "All B are A."

Try it — All cats are animals. Some animals are dogs. → Some cats are dogs. Valid?
Invalid. There's a valid diagram where the dog-circle never touches the cat-circle.

Coding-decoding

Understand it: codes are just consistent functions

A coding rule is a fixed transformation applied to every letter the same way (shift by a fixed number, reverse the alphabet, etc). Once you identify that rule from an example, applying it to a new word is pure mechanical substitution — the "puzzle" part is only in figuring out the rule, not in applying it.

Fix

Test your hypothesized rule against every given letter in the example before applying it to a new word.

Try it — CAT → DBU. How is DOG coded?
Rule: each letter +1. D→E, O→P, G→H → EPH.

Number & letter series

Understand it: patterns have a small number of "shapes"

Almost all series questions fall into a handful of underlying patterns: constant difference, constant ratio, a difference-of-differences (second-order pattern), two interleaved sequences, or squares/cubes with an offset. Checking these in a fixed order — rather than guessing randomly — turns pattern recognition into a systematic search instead of a lucky guess.

Try it — 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, ___
Each term = sum of previous two. 8+13 = 21.
Section four

Exam-day strategy

Triage rule

First pass: attempt only what you can solve in under 60 seconds. Mark everything else for a second pass.

90-second rule

If a question crosses 90 seconds with no clear path, abandon it — one hard question can cost you 2–3 easy ones elsewhere.

Negative marking

Only guess after eliminating at least 2 of 4 options — random full guesses are negative expected value under most schemes.

Order of attack

  1. Arithmetic-based quant (percentages, P&L, averages, SI/CI) — fastest, highest accuracy.
  2. Verbal — vocabulary and grammar (quick, low ambiguity).
  3. Logical reasoning — coding-decoding, series (fast pattern recognition).
  4. RC and para-jumbles (time-intensive but high question payoff).
  5. Puzzles, seating arrangements, and DI sets last — highest time risk.

The silly-mistake recap — reread this 10 minutes before the exam

  • % change base is always the old value.
  • Discount % is on Marked Price; Profit % is on Cost Price.
  • Efficiency and time are inversely related.
  • Compounding frequency changes both R and T.
  • "Some" never implies "Some are not" in syllogisms.
  • Circular seating left/right depends on facing direction.
  • Cone slant height ≠ vertical height.
